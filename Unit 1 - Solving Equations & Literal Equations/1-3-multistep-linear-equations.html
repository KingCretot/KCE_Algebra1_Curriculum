Let's Begin

Real problems rarely hand you a one-step equation.

Two friends split a taxi. The meter starts at $4, then adds $2.75 per mile, and they agree to split the total cost evenly, $18 each. How many miles did they ride? That question turns into an equation with several moving parts — not a one-step solve. Today we build the toolkit to handle that.

You already know how to undo one operation at a time. Today's job is stacking those moves together, in the right order, to solve equations with several steps — distributing, combining like terms, and clearing the equation down to one variable.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Combine like terms and use the distributive property inside an equation
  • 2Solve a multi-step one-variable equation in the correct order
  • 3Write and solve your own equation from a real-world word problem

Vocabulary

Two moves you'll use constantly

Like Terms

Like terms have the exact same variable (or no variable at all). You can add or subtract them together into one term.

💡Example: 3x and 2x are like terms — combine to 5x. 3x and 2 are NOT like terms — one has a variable, one doesn't.
Distributive Property

The distributive property lets you multiply a number by every term inside a set of parentheses.

💡Example: 3(x + 4) becomes 3x + 12 — the 3 multiplies both the x and the 4.

How It Works

The order that always works

Multi-step equations look intimidating only because there are several moves in a row. Do them in this order and it stays manageable every time:

  • 1Distribute — clear out any parentheses first
  • 2Combine like terms — on each side of the equation, separately
  • 3Move variable terms to one side, number terms to the other
  • 4Undo addition/subtraction, then undo multiplication/division last

💭 Notice the pattern: this is the exact reverse of how you'd build an expression up from x — multiply first, then add. To undo it, you subtract first, then divide. That reverse order is the whole secret.

Worked Example

Let's do one together, step by step

Problem: Solve 3(x + 4) − 2x = 20

1
Distribute the 3
3x + 12 − 2x = 20
2
Combine like terms (3x and −2x)
x + 12 = 20
3
Undo the addition
Subtract 12 from both sides: x = 8
4
Check the answer
3(8 + 4) − 2(8) = 3(12) − 16 = 36 − 16 = 20. Correct!

Real-World Context

Turning a word problem into an equation

Back to the taxi ride: the meter starts at $4, adds $2.75 per mile, and the total is split evenly between two friends at $18 each.

Turning words into an equation means translating one phrase at a time:

  • "Starts at $4" → a constant, 4
  • "$2.75 per mile" → a coefficient on the unknown number of miles, 2.75m
  • "Split evenly, $18 each" → the total was 2 × 18 = 36
2.75m + 4 = 36

Once it's an equation, it's just multi-step solving from here — the hard part was the translation, not the algebra.

Worked Example · Real-World

Solving the taxi problem

Problem: Solve 2.75m + 4 = 36 for m, the number of miles.

1
Undo the addition
Subtract 4 from both sides: 2.75m = 32
2
Undo the multiplication
Divide both sides by 2.75: m = 32 / 2.75 ≈ 11.6
3
Interpret it in context
The ride was about 11.6 miles — and that number only means something because we translated the words correctly first.

Why This Matters

Word problems are where algebra becomes real

Multi-step equations are the bridge between "doing algebra" and "using algebra." Every unit after this one — inequalities, systems, quadratics — leans on exactly this skill: turn a real situation into an equation, then solve it in order.

This shows up everywhere

  • Budgeting: fixed costs + a per-unit rate hitting a target total.
  • Splitting a bill, a paycheck, or shared expenses evenly.
  • Any "how many/how much" question where the setup has more than one moving part.

The equation-solving part is mechanical once you know the order. The real skill is the translation — and that only gets stronger with practice.
