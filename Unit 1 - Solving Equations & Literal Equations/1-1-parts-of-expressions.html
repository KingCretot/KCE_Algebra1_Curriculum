Let's Begin

An equation isn't just numbers — it's a sentence.

Think about the sentence “the dog ran fast.” It has parts: a subject (the dog), a verb (ran), a description (fast). An equation works the same way — every piece has a job, and once you can name the pieces, you can read what the equation is actually saying.

This year, almost everything you do in Algebra 1 starts with being able to look at an expression or equation and immediately see what it's built from. That's the whole job today.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Name the parts of an expression: terms, coefficients, variables, constants, factors
  • 2Point to a specific part of a real-world formula and say what it means
  • 3Treat a group of symbols as a single "chunk" when that's useful

Vocabulary · Part 1

The building blocks: term and coefficient

Term

A term is one piece of an expression, separated from other pieces by a + or − sign.

💡Example: in 5x + 3, there are two terms: 5x and 3.
Coefficient

A coefficient is the number multiplied in front of a variable in a term.

💡Example: in the term 5x, the coefficient is 5. In −2y, the coefficient is −2.

Vocabulary · Part 2

Three more parts: variable, constant, factor

Variable

A variable is a letter that stands in for a number that can change or is unknown.

💡Example: in 5x + 3, x is the variable — its value isn't fixed.
Constant

A constant is a term that's just a number — no variable attached, so its value never changes.

💡Example: in 5x + 3, the 3 is the constant.
Factor

A factor is anything being multiplied. A term can have more than one factor.

💡Example: in the term 5x, the factors are 5 and x — they're multiplied together.

How It Works

Labeling every part of an expression

Let's take one expression and label every single piece of it, all at once.

7x − 4

Two terms: 7x and −4. The coefficient is 7. The variable is x. The constant is −4. The factors of the first term are 7 and x.

Sometimes a whole chunk acts like one piece

Look at 3(x + 2). Here, (x + 2) isn't broken into separate terms yet — the whole thing in parentheses is being treated as one single factor, multiplied by 3. Being able to see a group like that as "one chunk" — instead of getting lost in the individual pieces — is one of the most useful skills in all of algebra.

💭 Quick check with yourself: in 2(y + 5), what's being treated as one single factor? — The whole quantity (y + 5) — it's one chunk, multiplied by 2.

Real-World Context

A formula is a real sentence with real parts

Derrick is using the formula P = 1000(1 + .1)t to predict the camel population in Australia over time.

Instead of treating this as "just symbols," Derrick can read it piece by piece:

  • 1000 is the starting camel population.
  • (1 + .1), or 1.1, is being treated as one single factor — the growth factor.
  • Reading that growth factor tells Derrick the population grows by 10% every year — without solving anything yet, just from naming the parts.

💭 This is the whole point of today: you don't have to solve an equation to get information out of it. Sometimes just identifying and interpreting a part answers the real question.

Worked Example

Let's do one together, step by step

Problem: A rideshare ride costs C = 2.50m + 4, where m is the number of miles driven. Interpret the parts of this formula.

1
Find the terms
Two terms, separated by the + sign: 2.50m and 4.
2
Interpret the coefficient
2.50 is the coefficient of m — in context, that's the cost per mile, $2.50.
3
Interpret the variable
m is the variable — the number of miles, which changes from ride to ride.
4
Interpret the constant
4 is the constant — a flat $4 fee charged no matter how many miles you go.

Why This Matters

Every formula you'll ever use has a story in its parts

From here on, almost every unit in Algebra 1 hands you a formula and expects you to make sense of it — interest formulas, distance formulas, cost formulas, growth formulas. None of them are scary once you can break them into terms, coefficients, variables, and constants, and ask what each piece means in that situation.

This shows up everywhere

  • A paycheck formula: hourly rate (coefficient) × hours (variable) + bonus (constant).
  • A gym membership: monthly fee (constant) + per-visit charge (coefficient × variable).
  • A savings account: starting balance (constant) growing by a percentage (part of a growth factor).

Once you can read the story inside a formula, formulas stop being something to memorize and start being something you understand.
