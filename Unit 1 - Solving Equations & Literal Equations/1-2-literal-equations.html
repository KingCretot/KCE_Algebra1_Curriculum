Let's Begin

Every formula can be rewritten to solve for any letter in it.

You already know the formula for the perimeter of a rectangle: P = 2l + 2w. That formula is built to find P. But what if you already know P and w, and need to find l instead? You don't need a new formula — you can rearrange the one you have.

An equation like this, with more than one variable, is called a literal equation. Today's entire job is learning how to solve a literal equation for whichever variable you actually need.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Explain what a literal equation is and why we rearrange them
  • 2Isolate a chosen variable using the same steps you use to solve any equation
  • 3Rearrange real formulas — temperature, geometry, and interest — to solve for a different quantity

Vocabulary

Two words for today

Literal Equation

A literal equation is an equation with two or more variables — usually a formula from science, geometry, or finance.

💡Example: P = 2l + 2w, A = πr², and d = rt are all literal equations.
Isolate a Variable

To isolate a variable means to rearrange the equation so that one chosen variable sits alone on one side, with everything else on the other side.

💡The tool you already have: the exact same inverse-operation steps you use to solve 3x + 5 = 20.

How It Works

The one rule that makes this easy

The rule: treat every other letter exactly like you'd treat a number. Undo operations in reverse order, using inverse operations, until your target variable is alone.

That's genuinely the whole idea. If you can solve 2x + 10 = 30 for x, you already have every skill you need to solve 2l + 2w = P for l — the letters w and P just ride along as if they were numbers.

Side-by-side, same steps

A
With a number
2x + 10 = 30 → subtract 10 → 2x = 20 → divide by 2 → x = 10
B
With a letter instead
2l + 2w = P → subtract 2w → 2l = P − 2w → divide by 2 → l = (P − 2w) / 2

💭 Notice: the answer isn't a number — it's an expression made of other letters. That's normal and expected for a literal equation.

Real-World Context

Why anyone actually does this

You'll meet literal equations most often in formulas for temperature, perimeter, area, volume, and interest. The reason to rearrange one is always the same: you know some of the letters, you need a different one, and you don't want to rebuild the formula from scratch every time.

Example: The Celsius-to-Fahrenheit formula is F = (9/5)C + 32. A recipe gives you an oven temperature in Celsius, but your oven only shows Fahrenheit. Rearranging this formula for C once means you can convert any Celsius reading to Fahrenheit forever after — not just this one recipe.

This is genuinely useful, not just a textbook exercise: engineers, nurses, chefs, and scientists rearrange formulas like this constantly, because the formula they're handed is rarely already solved for the exact thing they need.

Worked Example · Temperature

Solving F = (9/5)C + 32 for C

Goal: Rearrange F = (9/5)C + 32 so it's solved for C instead of F.

1
Undo the addition first
Subtract 32 from both sides: F − 32 = (9/5)C
2
Undo the multiplication
(9/5) is multiplying C. Multiply both sides by the reciprocal, (5/9): (5/9)(F − 32) = C
3
Write it solved for C
C = (5/9)(F − 32)
4
Check it makes sense
Try F = 212 (boiling point): C = (5/9)(212 − 32) = (5/9)(180) = 100°C. Correct!

Worked Example · Interest

Solving the compound interest formula for P

Goal: Given A = P(1 + r/n)nt, solve for P — the original amount deposited.

1
Identify what's multiplying P
The whole quantity (1 + r/n)nt is a single factor multiplying P.
2
Undo the multiplication
Divide both sides by that entire factor: A / (1 + r/n)nt = P
3
Write it solved for P
P = A / (1 + r/n)nt

💭 Same rule as before: the messy-looking (1 + r/n)nt is still just "one thing multiplying P" — treat the whole chunk as a single factor, same as Lesson 1.1.

Why This Matters

One formula, solved for anything you need

Once a formula is rearranged, it stays rearranged — you don't redo the algebra every time, you just plug numbers in. This is exactly how the Kelvin scale formula works: K = C + 273.15. Rearranged once for C, it becomes C = K − 273.15 — and now converting any Kelvin reading to Celsius is instant.

This shows up everywhere

  • Volume formulas rearranged to solve for height, radius, or a missing side.
  • Distance formula d = rt rearranged for time: t = d/r.
  • Simple interest I = Prt rearranged for the rate: r = I/(Pt).

Learning to rearrange a formula once means you never need to memorize a second version of it.
