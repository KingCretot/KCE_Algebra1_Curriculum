Let's Begin

Unit 3 read lines. Unit 4 writes them.

Last unit, you were handed a linear function and had to describe it. Now you'll flip the direction: given a graph, a table, or a written description, you'll build the equation yourself — in whichever form fits the information you're given.

There are three common forms for a linear equation, and today you'll learn which one to reach for depending on what you're handed.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Write a linear equation in slope-intercept form from a graph or table
  • 2Write a linear equation in point-slope form from two points or a point and a rate
  • 3Choose the right form based on what information you're given

Vocabulary

Three forms of the same line

Slope-Intercept Form

y = mx + b, where m is the slope and b is the y-intercept. Use this when you know the slope and where the line crosses the y-axis.

💡Best for: tables and graphs where the y-intercept is easy to see.
Point-Slope Form

y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), where m is the slope and (x₁, y₁) is any known point on the line.

💡Best for: when you have a slope and any single point, even if it's not the y-intercept.

How It Works

Two decisions before you write anything

Step 1: find the slope, however you can. From two points: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁). From a graph, count rise over run. From a table, use consecutive rows.

A
If you can see the y-intercept
Use y = mx + b. Plug in the slope for m and the y-intercept for b directly.
B
If you only have a point that isn't the y-intercept
Use y − y₁ = m(x − x₁) with the slope and that point, then simplify to slope-intercept form if needed.

💭 Notice: both forms describe the exact same line — you're choosing the form that's easiest to write given what you were handed, not choosing a different equation.

Real-World Context

Why you'd ever need to write the equation yourself

Real data rarely arrives as a ready-made formula. A business tracks sales for a few months and wants to predict next quarter. A scientist records a few temperature readings and wants a model. In both cases, you have points — not an equation — and writing the equation is the actual analytical work.

Example: A phone plan charged $35 after 2 GB and $50 after 5 GB used. There's no equation handed to you — you have to build one from those two data points to predict the cost at any other usage.

Worked Example · From Two Points

Writing an equation from (2, 35) and (5, 50)

Goal: Write the phone plan's cost equation using the two known points.

1
Find the slope
m = (50 − 35) / (5 − 2) = 15 / 3 = 5
2
Use point-slope form with one point
y − 35 = 5(x − 2)
3
Simplify to slope-intercept form
y − 35 = 5x − 10 → y = 5x + 25
4
Interpret it
$25 base fee, plus $5 per GB used.

Worked Example · From a Table

Writing an equation from a table

Table: x = 0, 1, 2, 3  →  y = 12, 9, 6, 3

1
Find the slope from the table
Each row, y drops by 3 while x rises by 1: m = −3
2
Read the y-intercept
At x = 0, y = 12 — this is already the y-intercept, no extra work needed.
3
Write the equation
y = −3x + 12

💭 Whenever x = 0 appears in the table, you get the y-intercept for free — skip point-slope form entirely and go straight to y = mx + b.

Why This Matters

Writing a line from data is real analysis

Reading a given equation is a starting skill. Building your own equation from raw data — two points, a table, a graph — is what turns you from someone who follows a formula into someone who can produce one. It's the core move behind trend lines, predictions, and models in every field that uses data.

This shows up everywhere

  • Business forecasting from a few months of sales data.
  • Science: building a model from a handful of experimental readings.
  • Personal finance: projecting savings growth from two account balances.
