Let's Begin

Two lines' slopes tell you their entire relationship to each other.

You already know how to write a line's equation from a slope and a point. Today adds one new wrinkle: writing the equation of a line that's parallel or perpendicular to a given line, through a given point — using one simple rule about how their slopes relate.

Once you know that rule, this is just Lesson 4.1's point-slope skill with an extra first step.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1State the slope rules for parallel and perpendicular lines
  • 2Write the equation of a line parallel to a given line, through a given point
  • 3Write the equation of a line perpendicular to a given line, through a given point

Vocabulary

Two relationships, two slope rules

Parallel Lines

Parallel lines have the exact same slope and never intersect. If one line has slope m, any line parallel to it also has slope m.

💡Example: y = 2x + 5 and y = 2x − 3 are parallel — same slope, different y-intercepts.
Perpendicular Lines

Perpendicular lines cross at a right angle. Their slopes are negative reciprocals — flip the fraction and change the sign.

💡Example: if one line has slope 2/3, a perpendicular line has slope −3/2.

How It Works

Find the new slope first, then finish like Lesson 4.1

1
Read the given line's slope
If it's in y = mx + b form, m is right there. If not, rearrange to find it.
2
Apply the rule
Parallel → keep the exact same slope. Perpendicular → flip the fraction and switch the sign.
3
Use point-slope form
With the new slope and the given point, write y − y₁ = m(x − x₁), then simplify.

💭 Notice: nothing about writing the final equation is new — step 3 is exactly Lesson 4.1. The only new skill today is step 2, finding the correct slope to use.

Real-World Context

Where this actually shows up

Parallel and perpendicular relationships aren't just a geometry curiosity — architects use perpendicular walls, city planners lay out perpendicular streets, and parallel lines describe things like two competing companies with identical growth rates but different starting points.

Example: A city's Main Street runs along y = 2x + 1. A new cross street must be built perpendicular to it, passing through the intersection point (4, 9). Finding that cross street's equation is exactly today's skill.

Worked Example · Parallel

A line parallel to y = 2x + 1, through (4, 9)

Goal: Write the equation of the line parallel to y = 2x + 1, passing through (4, 9).

1
Read the given slope
The given line has slope 2. A parallel line keeps slope 2.
2
Apply point-slope form
y − 9 = 2(x − 4)
3
Simplify
y − 9 = 2x − 8 → y = 2x + 1

💭 Interesting coincidence to check for: if your final equation exactly matches the original line, double-check the given point wasn't already ON the original line — it should be a different line unless stated otherwise.

Worked Example · Perpendicular

A line perpendicular to y = 2x + 1, through (4, 9)

Goal: Write the equation of the line perpendicular to y = 2x + 1, passing through (4, 9).

1
Read the given slope
The given line has slope 2, which can be written as 2/1.
2
Flip and switch the sign
2/1 flips to 1/2, then switches sign to −1/2. The perpendicular slope is −1/2.
3
Apply point-slope form and simplify
y − 9 = −½(x − 4) → y − 9 = −½x + 2 → y = −½x + 11

Why This Matters

One rule, two useful relationships

Recognizing parallel and perpendicular slopes at a glance is a skill that keeps paying off — not just in later geometry-flavored Algebra 1 problems, but in geometry class itself, and in any design or engineering field where right angles and consistent spacing matter.

This shows up everywhere

  • Architecture and construction: perpendicular walls, parallel floor joists.
  • City planning: street grids, often built from parallel and perpendicular roads.
  • Design: aligning elements on a page or screen using perpendicular guides.
