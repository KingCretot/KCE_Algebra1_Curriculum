Let's Begin

Interest on your interest

Two friends each put $1,000 away for 30 years at 6%. One earns simple interest: $60 a year, every year, for a final balance of $2,800. The other earns interest compounded yearly, so the interest itself earns interest, for a final balance of about $5,743. Same deposit, same rate — the only difference is how the interest is calculated.

Today you'll calculate both kinds of interest and see why one is a linear function and the other is exponential — the exact link between this unit and Units 3 and 4.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Calculate simple interest with I = Prt
  • 2Calculate compound interest with A = P(1 + r/n)nt
  • 3Explain why simple interest grows linearly and compound interest grows exponentially

Vocabulary

Two ways to earn interest

Simple Interest

Interest earned only on the original amount, the principal. I = Prt, where P is the principal, r is the yearly rate as a decimal, and t is the time in years.

💡Example: $500 at 4% for 3 years: I = 500(0.04)(3) = $60.
Compound Interest

Interest earned on the principal and on interest already earned. A = P(1 + r/n)nt, where n is the number of times interest is added per year.

💡Example: $500 at 4% compounded yearly for 3 years: A = 500(1.04)3 ≈ $562.43.

How It Works

Pick the formula, then plug in carefully

1
Convert the rate
Write the percent as a decimal: 5% → 0.05.
2
Simple interest
I = Prt gives the interest; the balance is P + I. The balance grows by the same amount, Pr, every year — that's linear.
3
Compound interest
A = P(1 + r/n)nt gives the balance directly. Yearly: n = 1. Quarterly: n = 4. Monthly: n = 12.
4
Compare
Simple interest adds a constant amount each year. Compound interest multiplies by a constant factor each period — that's exponential.

💭 Notice: Simple interest: A = P + Prt is a line with slope Pr. Compounded yearly: A = P(1 + r)t is exponential with factor 1 + r.

Real-World Context

Interest works both ways

Interest makes savings grow — and it makes debt grow, too. Credit cards and many loans compound interest, often monthly, so an unpaid balance grows exponentially. Knowing the formulas shows you the real cost of borrowing and the real reward of saving early.

Example: A $1,200 credit card balance at 24% yearly interest, compounded monthly, with no payments: A = 1200(1 + 0.24/12)12 = 1200(1.02)12 ≈ $1,521.89 after one year — more than $320 in interest.

Worked Example · Simple Interest

Simple interest on a $2,500 deposit

Goal: Find the interest and the balance on $2,500 at 3% simple interest for 4 years.

1
Identify the values
P = 2500, r = 0.03, t = 4
2
Apply I = Prt
I = 2500(0.03)(4) = $300
3
Find the balance
A = 2500 + 300 = $2,800. The balance rises $75 every year — a straight line with slope 75.

Worked Example · Compound Interest

The same deposit, compounded quarterly

Goal: Find the balance on $2,500 at 3% compounded quarterly for 4 years.

1
Identify the values
P = 2500, r = 0.03, n = 4, t = 4
2
Set up the formula
A = 2500(1 + 0.03/4)4 · 4 = 2500(1.0075)16
3
Evaluate
(1.0075)16 ≈ 1.12699, so A ≈ $2,817.48 — about $17 more than simple interest.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

$800 is invested at 5% interest for 2 years.

Question 1 — With simple interest, how much interest is earned?

Here's why: I = Prt = 800(0.05)(2) = $80. $40 is just one year of interest; the money is invested for 2 years.

Question 2 — With interest compounded yearly, what is the balance?

Here's why: A = 800(1.05)2 = 800(1.1025) = $882. That's $2 more than with simple interest ($880), because in year 2 the first year's $40 earns interest too.

Why This Matters

The math behind every money decision

Savings accounts, student loans, car loans, and credit cards all run on these two formulas. Knowing which kind of interest you're dealing with — and how often it compounds — tells you whether time is working for you or against you.

This shows up everywhere

  • Choosing a savings account by its rate and how often it compounds.
  • Understanding why paying off a credit card early saves so much.
  • Comparing loan offers before borrowing.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
What is the simple interest on $1,500 at 4% for 5 years?
Here's why: I = Prt = 1500(0.04)(5) = $300. $1,800 is the final balance (principal plus interest), not the interest.
Question 2
$1,000 is invested at 6% compounded yearly. Which expression gives the balance after 10 years?
Here's why: Compounded yearly, A = P(1 + r)t = 1000(1 + 0.06)10. Using 0.06 alone as the factor would shrink the money; the factor must be 1 + r.
Question 3
In A = P(1 + r/n)nt, what is n when interest is compounded monthly?
Here's why: n is the number of times interest is added each year. Monthly means 12 times; quarterly would be 4, and daily would be 365.
Question 4
Why does simple interest produce a linear graph?
Here's why: Simple interest adds Pr dollars every year — a constant rate of change, which is what makes a function linear. Multiplying by the same factor each year is what compound interest does.
Question 5
$2,000 earns 5% interest compounded yearly. How much is in the account after 3 years?
Here's why: A = 2000(1.05)3 = 2000(1.157625) = $2,315.25. Simple interest would give $2,300; the extra $15.25 is interest earned on earlier interest.
