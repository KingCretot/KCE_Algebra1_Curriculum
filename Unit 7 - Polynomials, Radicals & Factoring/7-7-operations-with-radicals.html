Let's Begin

Like radicals behave like like terms

You already know 3x + 5x = 8x. Swap x for √2 and the same rule holds: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2. But √2 + √3 doesn't combine, just like x + y doesn't. Radicals follow the same rules as variables — with one extra skill: simplifying.

Today you'll simplify square roots and cube roots, then add, subtract, multiply, and divide them.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Simplify a square root or cube root using perfect-square or perfect-cube factors
  • 2Add and subtract like radicals
  • 3Multiply and divide radicals, then simplify the result

Vocabulary

Simplest form and like radicals

Simplest Radical Form

A square root is simplified when no perfect-square factor other than 1 remains under the radical. For a cube root, no perfect-cube factor remains.

💡Example: √50 = √(25 · 2) = 5√2.
Like Radicals

Radicals with the same index and the same number under the radical. Only like radicals can be added or subtracted.

💡Example: 4√3 and −√3 are like radicals; √3 and √5 are not.

How It Works

Simplify first, then operate

1
Simplify each radical
Pull out the largest perfect-square factor: √72 = √(36 · 2) = 6√2.
2
Add or subtract like radicals
Combine the coefficients and keep the radical: 6√2 + 3√2 = 9√2.
3
Multiply
√a · √b = √(ab), then simplify: √6 · √3 = √18 = 3√2.
4
Divide
√a ÷ √b = √(a ÷ b): √48 ÷ √3 = √16 = 4. Cube roots follow the same rules.

💭 Notice: √(a + b) is not √a + √b. For example, √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, but √9 + √16 = 7.

Real-World Context

Exact answers

Builders and engineers keep answers in radical form so they stay exact until the final measurement. The diagonal of a square with side s is s√2, and lining up diagonals is radical addition.

Example: Two square tiles have sides of 3 and 5 inches. Their diagonals are 3√2 and 5√2 inches, so laid end to end they measure 8√2 ≈ 11.3 inches.

Worked Example · Adding

Simplifying √12 + √75

Goal: Add and write in simplest radical form.

1
Simplify √12
√(4 · 3) = 2√3
2
Simplify √75
√(25 · 3) = 5√3
3
Add the like radicals
2√3 + 5√3 = 7√3

💭 Notice: Before simplifying, √12 and √75 don't look alike. Simplifying first is what shows they can be added.

Worked Example · Multiplying & Cube Roots

Multiplying √8 · √6 and ∛4 · ∛16

Goal: Multiply and simplify each product.

1
Square roots: multiply under one radical
√8 · √6 = √48
2
Simplify
√48 = √(16 · 3) = 4√3
3
Cube roots work the same way
∛4 · ∛16 = ∛64 = 4, because 4 · 4 · 4 = 64

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Simplify: √50 − √18

Question 1 — What are √50 and √18 in simplest form?

Here's why: √50 = √(25 · 2) = 5√2 and √18 = √(9 · 2) = 3√2. The perfect square comes out as its square root: √25 = 5, not 25.

Question 2 — What is √50 − √18?

Here's why: 5√2 − 3√2 = 2√2. Subtracting under the radical (√(50 − 18) = √32 = 4√2) isn't allowed, and it gives a different number.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Simplify √98.
Here's why: 98 = 49 · 2, and √49 = 7, so √98 = 7√2.
Question 2
Simplify 4√5 + 2√5.
Here's why: These are like radicals, so add the coefficients: 4 + 2 = 6, keeping √5. The radical part doesn't change when you add.
Question 3
Simplify √27 + √12.
Here's why: √27 = 3√3 and √12 = 2√3, so the sum is 5√3. √39 adds under the radical, which isn't allowed.
Question 4
Multiply √10 · √5.
Here's why: √10 · √5 = √50 = √(25 · 2) = 5√2. √15 adds the numbers instead of multiplying them.
Question 5
Simplify ∛54 ÷ ∛2.
Here's why: ∛54 ÷ ∛2 = ∛27 = 3, because 3 · 3 · 3 = 27. 27 is the number under the radical, not the answer.
