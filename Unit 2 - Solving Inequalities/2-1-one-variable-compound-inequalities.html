Let's Begin

Not every real-world answer is a single number.

A roller coaster doesn't say "you must be exactly 52 inches tall." It says "you must be at least 48 inches tall." A budget doesn't say "spend exactly $200." It says "spend at most $200." Real life is full of ranges, not single answers — and inequalities are how algebra handles that.

Everything you already know about solving equations still applies here — with one new rule to watch for.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Solve a one-variable linear inequality and graph its solution on a number line
  • 2Know exactly when — and why — you must flip the inequality sign
  • 3Solve a compound inequality (a range with two bounds) at once

Vocabulary · Part 1

Reading the symbols

Strict Inequality

< means "less than" and > means "greater than." The boundary value itself is NOT included in the solution.

💡Graphed as: an OPEN circle on the number line — the boundary is a hole, not a solution.
Inclusive Inequality

≤ means "less than or equal to" and ≥ means "greater than or equal to." The boundary value IS included.

💡Graphed as: a CLOSED (filled-in) circle — the boundary counts as a solution.

Vocabulary · Part 2

The one rule that's different from equations

The flip rule: if you multiply or divide BOTH sides of an inequality by a NEGATIVE number, you must flip the direction of the inequality symbol.

Here's why, with real numbers: start with the true statement 2 < 5. Multiply both sides by −1: you'd get −2 < −5, which is FALSE (−2 is actually bigger than −5). Flipping the symbol fixes it: −2 > −5 — true.

💭 When do you flip? Only when you multiply or divide by a negative. Adding or subtracting any number — positive or negative — never flips the sign.

Compound Inequality — a single statement combining two inequalities to describe a range with both a lower and upper bound, like 2 < x ≤ 7 ("x is between 2 and 7, not including 2, up to and including 7").

Worked Example

Solving an inequality that needs the flip

Problem: Solve −3x + 5 < 20

1
Undo the addition
Subtract 5 from both sides: −3x < 15
2
Undo the multiplication — and flip!
Divide both sides by −3 (a negative number), so flip < to >: x > −5
3
Check it makes sense
Try x = 0 (should work, since 0 > −5): −3(0) + 5 = 5, and 5 < 20. Correct! Try x = −5 (should NOT work — it's the open boundary): −3(−5) + 5 = 20, and 20 < 20 is false. Correct — open circle at −5.

Worked Example · Compound

Solving a compound inequality

Problem: Solve −2 < 3x + 4 ≤ 13

1
Do the same operation to all THREE parts
Subtract 4 from all three parts: −6 < 3x ≤ 9
2
Divide all three parts by 3 (positive — no flip needed)
−2 < x ≤ 3
3
Graph it
Open circle at −2 (not included), closed circle at 3 (included), shaded line connecting them.

Real-World Context

Where compound inequalities show up

A water park's height sign reads: "Riders must be at least 48 inches tall, but shorter than 72 inches, for this slide."

Translating this piece by piece:

  • "At least 48 inches" → h ≥ 48 (48 is included)
  • "Shorter than 72 inches" → h < 72 (72 is NOT included)
  • Combined into one compound inequality: 48 ≤ h < 72

Notice the two boundaries can use different symbols — one inclusive, one strict — in the very same compound inequality. Read each phrase on its own first, then combine.

Why This Matters

Ranges are everywhere once you look for them

Almost nothing in real life needs an exact number — it needs a boundary. A speed limit is a maximum. A minimum wage is a minimum. A healthy resting heart rate is a range. Being able to solve for a boundary, not just a single answer, is what makes inequalities so useful.

This shows up everywhere

  • Speed limits, minimum ages, maximum capacities.
  • Budgets: spending must stay at or under a limit.
  • Grading: a score range that earns a certain letter grade.

The algebra is nearly identical to equations — the flip rule is the only genuinely new thing to remember.
