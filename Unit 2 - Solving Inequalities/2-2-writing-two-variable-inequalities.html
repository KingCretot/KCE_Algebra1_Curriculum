Let's Begin

What if the answer isn't a line — it's everything on one SIDE of a line?

The equation y = 2x + 1 describes every point exactly ON that line. But the inequality y < 2x + 1 describes every point BELOW that line — a whole region, not just a line. Today you'll learn to write that kind of inequality from a real situation or a description of a graph.

This builds directly on Lesson 2.1 — same inequality symbols, same flip rule — just applied to relationships between two variables instead of one.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Write a two-variable inequality from a real-world description
  • 2Write a two-variable inequality from a description of a graph
  • 3Decide whether the boundary line should be solid or dashed

Vocabulary

Two new terms for two-variable inequalities

Boundary Line

The boundary line is the line you get by replacing the inequality symbol with an equals sign. It's the edge that separates the solutions from the non-solutions.

💡Example: for y ≤ 2x + 1, the boundary line is y = 2x + 1.
Solid vs. Dashed

The boundary is drawn solid when the symbol is ≤ or ≥ (boundary included). It's drawn dashed when the symbol is < or > (boundary NOT included).

💡Same idea as Lesson 2.1's open vs. closed circle — just for a whole line instead of one point.

How It Works

Writing from a word problem

The process has two parts: figure out the equation the situation is built on, then decide which inequality symbol the words are describing.

1
Find the relationship (the equation)
Identify the two quantities and how they combine — this becomes your boundary line.
2
Translate the limiting words
"At most," "no more than," "cannot exceed" → ≤.   "At least," "no less than," "minimum" → ≥.   "More than," "fewer than" (no equal option) → > or <.

💭 Watch the wording carefully: "at most" and "no more than" both mean ≤ — they include the boundary. "Fewer than" does NOT include the boundary, so it's a strict <.

Worked Example · Word Problem

A fundraiser ticket problem

Problem: Adult tickets cost $8 and student tickets cost $5. The class needs to raise at least $400. Write an inequality relating the number of adult tickets (x) and student tickets (y) sold.

1
Find the relationship
Total money raised: 8x + 5y
2
Translate "at least"
"At least $400" means the total must be $400 or more — that's ≥
3
Write the inequality
8x + 5y ≥ 400

Worked Example · From a Graph

Writing from a description of a graph

Problem: A graph shows a DASHED line through (0, −1) with a slope of 2. The region ABOVE the line is shaded. Write the inequality.

1
Write the boundary line's equation
Slope 2, y-intercept −1: y = 2x − 1
2
Dashed line → strict inequality
Dashed means the boundary is NOT included, so the symbol will be < or > (not ≤ or ≥)
3
Shaded above → greater than
"Above" the line means y-values are bigger than the line's y-values for the same x — that's >
4
Write the inequality
y > 2x − 1

Real-World Context

Why two variables at once?

A caterer has a rule: for every event, the number of vegetarian meals (v) plus the number of standard meals (s) can't exceed the kitchen's capacity of 120 meals in one night.

Translating: "can't exceed 120" → ≤ 120, so the inequality is v + s ≤ 120. This single inequality describes every possible valid combination of vegetarian and standard meals at once — far more useful than checking each combination one at a time.

Two-variable inequalities are how businesses model constraints with more than one moving part — budgets, capacities, ingredient limits — all at once.

Why This Matters

Setting up the constraint is the hard part

Once you can correctly write a two-variable inequality, graphing it (next lesson) is mostly mechanical. The real skill — the one businesses, engineers, and planners actually need — is translating a real constraint into the right inequality with the right symbol.

This shows up everywhere

  • Budget constraints: total spending across two categories.
  • Capacity limits: two types of items sharing one limited space.
  • Nutrition or mixture limits: combining two ingredients within a bound.

Get the wording translation right, and the rest of this unit builds smoothly on top of it.
