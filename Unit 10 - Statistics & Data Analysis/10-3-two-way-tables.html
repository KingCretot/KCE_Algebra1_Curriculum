Let's Begin

Counting two things at once

A survey asks 100 students two questions: do you play a sport, and do you have a part-time job? A single list can't show how the answers connect. A two-way table can — and it shows whether athletes are more or less likely to work.

Today you'll build two-way frequency tables, compute relative frequencies, and use them to decide whether two categorical variables are associated.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Build a two-way frequency table from categorical data
  • 2Find joint, marginal, and conditional relative frequencies
  • 3Use conditional relative frequencies to judge association

Vocabulary

Three kinds of frequency

Joint and Marginal Frequency

A joint frequency is a count inside the table (both conditions). A marginal frequency is a row or column total.

💡Example: "plays a sport and has a job" is joint; "all students with jobs" is marginal.
Conditional Relative Frequency

A joint count divided by its row or column total: the fraction of one group that has a characteristic.

💡Example: 10 of 40 athletes have jobs, so 10/40 = 25%.

How It Works

Build it, total it, compare

1
Set up rows and columns
One variable's categories across the top, the other's down the side, plus a total row and a total column.
2
Fill in and total
Every row and column must add up, and the grand total is the number surveyed.
3
Find relative frequencies
Divide by the grand total (joint) or by a row or column total (conditional).
4
Compare conditional percentages
If the percentages differ a lot between groups, the variables are associated.

💭 Notice: Compare percentages, not raw counts. 20 of 40 and 30 of 60 are both 50% — the same rate.

Real-World Context

Spotting links between categories

Researchers and businesses use two-way tables to check whether two categories are linked: product preference by age group, or study method and passing rate.

Example: A store finds 45 of 60 teen customers prefer its app (75%), but only 20 of 80 adult customers do (25%). The big gap suggests app preference is associated with age group.

Worked Example · Building the Table

Sport and job survey of 100 students

Goal: 40 students play a sport, and 10 of them have a job. 30 of the 60 students who don't play a sport have a job. Build the table.

1
Fill in the given counts
Sport and job: 10. No sport and job: 30. Row totals: 40 and 60.
2
Complete by subtraction
Sport, no job: 40 − 10 = 30. No sport, no job: 60 − 30 = 30.
3
Total the columns
Job: 10 + 30 = 40. No job: 30 + 30 = 60. Grand total: 100 ✓

Worked Example · Conditional Frequencies

Are sports and jobs associated?

Goal: Compare the job rates of athletes and non-athletes.

1
Athletes with jobs
10 ÷ 40 = 25%
2
Non-athletes with jobs
30 ÷ 60 = 50%
3
Interpret
Non-athletes are twice as likely to have a job, so the variables are associated. That doesn't prove playing a sport stops students from working — more on that in Lesson 10.6.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Use the table: sport and job 10, sport and no job 30, no sport and job 30, no sport and no job 30.

Question 1 — What percent of all 100 students play a sport and have no job?

Here's why: That's a joint relative frequency: 30 out of the grand total of 100 is 30%. 75% is 30 out of the 40 athletes, a conditional frequency.

Question 2 — Of the students with jobs, what percent play a sport?

Here's why: Condition on the job column: 10 athletes out of 40 students with jobs = 25%. 10% divides by the grand total instead.

Why This Matters

The tool for categorical relationships

Two-way tables are the main tool for spotting relationships between categorical variables. The EOC asks you to complete them, compute conditional percentages, and interpret them.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
In a two-way table, what is a marginal frequency?
Here's why: Marginal frequencies sit in the margins: the row and column totals.
Question 2
50 students were surveyed. 30 prefer dogs and 20 prefer cats. 18 of the dog lovers and 12 of the cat lovers are 9th graders. How many 9th graders were surveyed?
Here's why: Add the 9th graders in both columns: 18 + 12 = 30. That's the marginal total for the 9th-grade row.
Question 3
In the same survey, what percent of the dog lovers are 9th graders?
Here's why: Condition on dog lovers: 18 ÷ 30 = 60%. 36% divides by the grand total of 50 instead.
Question 4
Of 80 people who exercise, 60 sleep well. Of 120 who don't exercise, 48 sleep well. Which is true?
Here's why: 60 ÷ 80 = 75% and 48 ÷ 120 = 40%. The rates differ a lot, so the variables are associated — but association alone doesn't prove cause.
Question 5
How can you tell from a two-way table that two variables are associated?
Here's why: Association means the rate of one characteristic changes from group to group, which you see by comparing conditional percentages.
