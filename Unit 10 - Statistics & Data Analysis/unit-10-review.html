Key Ideas · Lessons 10.1–10.3

One variable and two categories

Lesson 10.1

Classify, then display

Numerical or categorical? Univariate or bivariate? One numerical → histogram, dot plot, or box plot. One categorical → bar chart. Two numerical → scatter plot. Two categorical → two-way table.

Example: Hours of practice and points scored: numerical, bivariate → scatter plot.
Lesson 10.2

Center, spread, shape

Five-number summary → box plot. IQR = Q3 − Q1. Outliers lie beyond Q1 − 1.5·IQR or Q3 + 1.5·IQR. Use the median and IQR for skewed data or data with outliers.

Example: 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 30: mean ≈ 9.3, median 6; 30 is an outlier.
Box plot: min, Q1, median, Q3, max
Lesson 10.3

Two-way tables

Joint frequency: a cell. Marginal: a total. Conditional: a cell ÷ its row or column total. Different conditional percentages → association.

Example: 10 of 40 athletes work (25%) vs. 30 of 60 non-athletes (50%): associated.

Key Ideas · Lessons 10.4–10.7

Two numerical variables, causation, and sampling

Lesson 10.4

Lines of fit

Fit a line through the trend. Slope = change in y per unit of x; y-intercept = predicted y at x = 0. Don't extrapolate far beyond the data.

Example: y ≈ 4.6x + 57.8: about 4.6 more points per hour of study.
Lesson 10.5

Correlation and residuals

r runs from −1 to 1: the sign gives direction, |r| near 1 means strong. Residual = actual − predicted. A random residual plot means the line fits.

Example: Predicted 85, actual 79 → residual −6, below the line.
Lesson 10.6

Correlation is not causation

Look for lurking variables. Only a randomized experiment can support a cause-and-effect claim.

Example: Shoe size and reading level are correlated because of age.
Lesson 10.7

Sampling and margin of error

Estimate from a random sample, scale up for totals, and report estimate ± margin of error. Bigger samples → smaller margins.

Example: 8 of 50 trees diseased → 16% of 2,000 ≈ 320 trees, 16% ± 10%.

Watch Out

The mistakes that cost the most points

The mistakeThe fix
Treating number labels as numerical dataZip codes and ID numbers are categorical: averaging them means nothing.
Using the mean when there's an outlierFor skewed data or outliers, report the median and IQR.
Comparing raw counts instead of percentagesCompare conditional percentages: 20 of 40 equals 30 of 60.
Extrapolating far beyond the dataPredict only within (or near) the range of the data.
Reading the sign of r as its strength−0.9 is stronger than 0.5; strength is |r|.
Getting residuals backwardResidual = actual − predicted, not predicted − actual.
Calling a correlation a causeWithout a randomized experiment, say "is associated with."
Trusting a biased sampleA sample must be random to represent the population.

Practice Test

10 questions, every lesson mixed together

Just like a unit test, these jump between lessons. You get two tries on each question, and every question explains itself after you answer.

Question 1
Which data set is categorical and bivariate?
Here's why: Both variables are labels, and each student gives two answers. Height and weight are bivariate but numerical.
Question 2
Data: 10, 12, 13, 13, 15, 16, 41. Which measure of center best represents a typical value?
Here's why: 41 is an outlier that pulls the mean up to about 17.1, higher than six of the seven values. The median, 13, is more typical.
Question 3
Q1 = 22 and Q3 = 30. Which value is an outlier?
Here's why: IQR = 8, so the fences are 22 − 12 = 10 and 30 + 12 = 42. Only 44 is beyond them.
Question 4
Of 60 juniors, 42 have a driver's license. Of 50 sophomores, 15 do. What percent of juniors have a license?
Here's why: Condition on juniors: 42 ÷ 60 = 70%. For sophomores it's only 30%, so grade level and having a license are associated.
Question 5
A line of fit for a phone's battery is B = −8h + 100, where h is hours of use. What does −8 mean?
Here's why: The slope is the change in B per hour of use: about 8 percentage points lost each hour. The starting charge is the intercept, 100%.
Question 6
Which r describes the weakest linear relationship?
Here's why: Strength depends on |r|. 0.12 is closest to 0, so it's the weakest.
Question 7
A model predicts 64, and the actual value is 59. What is the residual?
Here's why: Residual = actual − predicted = 59 − 64 = −5. The point is below the line.
Question 8
Cities with more coffee shops have more traffic jams. What is a likely lurking variable?
Here's why: Larger cities have more coffee shops and more traffic. Population drives both.
Question 9
A random sample of 120 of 3,000 voters finds 54 support a measure. Estimate the number of supporters.
Here's why: 54 ÷ 120 = 0.45, and 0.45 × 3,000 = 1,350.
Question 10
A poll reports 47% ± 5%. Which statement is correct?
Here's why: Subtract and add the margin of error: 47 − 5 = 42 and 47 + 5 = 52. The true value could be on either side of 50%.
