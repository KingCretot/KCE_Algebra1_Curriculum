Let's Begin

Learning about many from a few

No one surveys all 330 million Americans before an election, yet polls estimate results within a few percentage points. The trick is a random sample — and an honest margin of error that says how far off the estimate might be.

Today you'll estimate a population total, mean, or percentage from a random sample, and use simulation to see how much a sample estimate can vary.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Use sample data to estimate a population mean, total, or percentage
  • 2Explain why a sample should be random
  • 3Develop and interpret a margin of error using simulation

Vocabulary

Samples and their uncertainty

Population and Sample

The population is everyone you want to know about. The sample is the part you actually measure. A random sample gives every individual an equal chance of being chosen.

💡Example: population: all 1,200 students; sample: 80 of them chosen at random.
Margin of Error

How far a sample estimate is likely to be from the true population value. Estimates are reported as estimate ± margin of error.

💡Example: 52% ± 3% means the true value is likely between 49% and 55%.

How It Works

Sample, estimate, measure the wobble

1
Take a random sample
Random selection avoids bias, like surveying only your friends.
2
Compute the sample statistic
The sample mean or the sample proportion (percentage).
3
Scale up to estimate a total
Population total ≈ sample proportion × population size.
4
Simulate to find a margin of error
Take many random samples, or simulate them, and record each result. About 95% of the results fall within the margin of error of the center.

💭 Notice: Larger random samples give smaller margins of error. But doubling the sample doesn't halve the margin; you need about four times the sample to cut it in half.

Real-World Context

Estimates everywhere

Polls, factory quality checks, and wildlife counts all estimate a whole population from a sample.

Example: In a random sample of 200 of a school's 1,500 students, 48 want a later start time. That's 24%, so an estimate for the school is 0.24 × 1,500 = 360 students. If simulations show sample percentages vary by about ±6%, the estimate is 24% ± 6%: roughly 270 to 450 students.

Worked Example · Estimating

Estimating a population total

Goal: A random sample of 50 of a farm's 2,000 apple trees finds 8 with a disease. Estimate how many trees are diseased.

1
Sample proportion
8 ÷ 50 = 0.16, or 16%
2
Scale up
0.16 × 2,000 = 320 trees
3
Interpret
About 320 trees are likely diseased. Because this is an estimate from a sample, the true number could be somewhat higher or lower.

Worked Example · Simulation

Building a margin of error

Goal: A computer drew 100 random samples of 50 trees each from an orchard where 16% are diseased, and recorded each sample's percentage.

1
Find the center
The sample percentages center on about 16%.
2
Find the spread
95 of the 100 results fell between 6% and 26% — within about 10 percentage points of the center.
3
State the margin of error
16% ± 10%: the true percentage is likely between 6% and 26%, or about 120 to 520 of the 2,000 trees.
100 simulated samples of 50 trees

💭 Notice: With only 50 trees per sample, the margin of error is wide. A sample of 200 trees would cut it roughly in half.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

A random sample of 80 students at a school of 1,000 finds 20 who walk to school.

Question 1 — What percent of the sample walks?

Here's why: 20 ÷ 80 = 0.25, or 25%. 20% treats the count of 20 as if it were already a percentage.

Question 2 — About how many students at the whole school walk?

Here's why: Scale up: 0.25 × 1,000 = 250. 20 is only the count in the sample.

Why This Matters

Honest estimates

Every poll and survey you read is an estimate from a sample. Knowing whether the sample was random, and how big the margin of error is, tells you how much to trust it.

This shows up everywhere

  • Reading election polls with their margins of error.
  • Quality control in manufacturing.
  • Estimating wildlife populations.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Why should a sample be chosen at random?
Here's why: Random selection gives every individual the same chance of being chosen, so the sample doesn't systematically favor one group.
Question 2
A poll reports 58% ± 4%. Which interval likely contains the true percentage?
Here's why: Subtract and add the margin: 58 − 4 = 54 and 58 + 4 = 62.
Question 3
A random sample of 40 of 600 phones finds 3 defective. Estimate the number of defective phones.
Here's why: 3 ÷ 40 = 0.075, and 0.075 × 600 = 45. 3 is only the count in the sample.
Question 4
How does a larger random sample affect the margin of error?
Here's why: Larger random samples vary less from sample to sample, so the margin of error shrinks — but it never reaches zero.
Question 5
A survey of only basketball team members finds 90% want a new gym. Why is this a poor estimate for the whole school?
Here's why: Team members are much more likely to want a new gym than a typical student, so this sample doesn't represent the school.
