Let's Begin

Ice cream and sunburns

Over a year, ice cream sales and sunburns rise and fall together — a strong positive correlation. Does ice cream cause sunburns? Of course not. Hot, sunny weather drives both. Spotting that hidden third factor is one of the most useful skills in all of statistics.

Today you'll separate correlation from causation, look for lurking variables, and learn what kind of study can actually show cause and effect.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Explain the difference between correlation and causation
  • 2Identify lurking variables
  • 3Recognize which kind of study can support a claim of causation

Vocabulary

Two words that change the claim

Causation

One variable directly produces a change in another. Only a well-designed experiment with random assignment can establish it.

💡Example: a randomized trial showing that a medicine lowers blood pressure.
Lurking Variable

A third variable that affects both variables in a study, creating a correlation between them even though neither causes the other.

💡Example: summer heat lurks behind both ice cream sales and sunburns.

How It Works

Question every link

1
Spot the claim
Is someone saying X is linked to Y, or that X causes Y?
2
Look for a lurking variable
Could something else drive both? Age, income, weather, and time are common culprits.
3
Check the type of study
Observational study (recording what happens) → association only. Randomized experiment → can support causation.
4
Word it carefully
Say "is associated with" unless an experiment supports "causes."

💭 Notice: Correlation can still be useful for prediction without causation. Ice cream sales really do predict sunburns — they just don't cause them.

Real-World Context

Reading headlines critically

Headlines often turn a correlation into a cause: "Students who eat breakfast get better grades." Before believing it, ask what else those students might have in common.

Example: Fires with more firefighters tend to cause more damage. The lurking variable is the size of the fire: bigger fires bring more firefighters and cause more damage.

Worked Example · Lurking Variables

Shoe size and reading level

Goal: Among elementary students, shoe size and reading level are strongly correlated. Do bigger feet improve reading?

1
Look for a third factor
Age (or grade level) affects both.
2
Explain the link
Older children have bigger feet, and they've also had more years of reading practice.
3
Conclude
The correlation is real, but it isn't causation. Age is the lurking variable.

Worked Example · Study Design

Observational study vs. experiment

Goal: Can each study show that a tutoring program raises test scores?

1
Study A
Compare students who chose tutoring with students who didn't. This is observational: motivated students may be the ones who choose tutoring, so motivation could be lurking. It shows association only.
2
Study B
Randomly assign 100 students to tutoring or no tutoring, then compare their scores. Random assignment balances motivation and other factors between the groups.
3
Conclude
Only Study B can support the claim that tutoring causes higher scores.

A study finds that people who carry lighters are more likely to develop lung disease.

Question 1 — What is a likely lurking variable?

Here's why: People who smoke are more likely to carry lighters and more likely to develop lung disease. Smoking drives both.

Here's why: The data show an association. The lurking variable, smoking, explains it, so there's no evidence that lighters themselves cause anything.

Why This Matters

The most useful question in statistics

"Correlation is not causation" may be the most useful idea in statistics. It protects you from misleading headlines, flawed advertising, and your own quick conclusions.

This shows up everywhere

  • Evaluating health and science news.
  • Judging advertising claims.
  • Designing a fair experiment for a science project.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
A strong correlation between two variables means…
Here's why: Correlation describes how two variables move together. Causation needs more evidence, usually a randomized experiment.
Question 2
Towns with more libraries have more crime. What is a likely lurking variable?
Here's why: Bigger towns have more libraries and more crime. Population drives both.
Question 3
Which kind of study can show causation?
Here's why: Random assignment balances lurking variables between the groups, so a difference in results can be credited to the treatment.
Question 4
Students who sleep more tend to have higher grades. Which statement is best supported?
Here's why: Without an experiment, the data support only an association. Lurking variables, such as schedules or stress, could explain the link.
Question 5
Why does random assignment matter in an experiment?
Here's why: When chance decides who gets the treatment, other factors tend to even out between the groups.
