Let's Begin

How well does the line fit?

Two scatter plots can both slope upward, but in one the points hug the line and in the other they're scattered widely. Correlation measures how tightly the points follow a line, and residuals measure exactly how far each point misses.

Today you'll describe a correlation's direction and strength, interpret r, and use residuals to judge whether a linear model fits.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe the direction and strength of a correlation
  • 2Interpret the correlation coefficient r
  • 3Calculate and interpret residuals and read a residual plot

Vocabulary

Two measures of fit

Correlation Coefficient (r)

A number from −1 to 1. Its sign gives the direction; the closer |r| is to 1, the stronger the linear relationship. r near 0 means little or no linear relationship.

💡Example: r = −0.92 is a strong negative correlation; r = 0.3 is weak and positive.
Residual

Observed y − predicted y. Positive: the point is above the line. Negative: it's below.

💡Example: actual 80, predicted 74 → residual +6.

How It Works

Direction, strength, residuals

1
Direction
Positive: y tends to rise as x rises. Negative: y tends to fall. None: no clear trend.
2
Strength
Points close to a line → strong (|r| near 1). Widely scattered → weak (|r| near 0).
3
Residuals
For each point, subtract the predicted value from the actual value.
4
Residual plot
Plot residuals against x. Random scatter around 0 means the linear model fits; a curved pattern means another model would fit better.

💭 Notice: A strong correlation describes how well a line fits. It doesn't tell you how steep the line is, and it never proves that one variable causes the other.

Real-World Context

Who does the model miss?

Researchers report correlation coefficients to show how strongly two measures are linked, and residuals show which individual cases a model over- or underestimates.

Example: A model predicts a home's price from its size. A home with a residual of +$40,000 sold for more than its size alone predicts — perhaps because of its location.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

A line of fit predicts a score of 85, and the student actually scored 79.

Question 1 — What is the residual?

Here's why: Residual = actual − predicted = 79 − 85 = −6.

Question 2 — What does a negative residual mean?

Here's why: A negative residual means the point lies below the line: the student scored lower than the model predicted.

Why This Matters

Judging a model, not just using it

Correlation and residuals let you judge a model before trusting it. A strong r and a patternless residual plot mean the line is a good summary; a curved residual plot sends you back to Unit 4's question of which model fits best.

This shows up everywhere

  • Deciding whether a linear model is trustworthy.
  • Finding the cases a model gets most wrong.
  • Reading research that reports r.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
Which r shows the strongest linear relationship?
Here's why: Strength depends on how close |r| is to 1, not on the sign. |−0.91| = 0.91 is the closest.
Question 2
What does r = 0.05 describe?
Here's why: r near 0 means the points don't follow a line at all.
Question 3
A model predicts 50, and the actual value is 56. What is the residual?
Here's why: Residual = actual − predicted = 56 − 50 = 6. Positive means the point is above the line.
Question 4
A residual plot shows a clear U-shaped pattern. What does that suggest?
Here's why: Residuals from a good linear fit look random. A curve means the data follow a curved pattern, so another model, such as a quadratic, would fit better.
Question 5
As hours of TV increase, test scores tend to decrease, and the points lie close to a line. Which r is most likely?
Here's why: Decreasing means negative, and close to a line means strong, so r should be near −1.
