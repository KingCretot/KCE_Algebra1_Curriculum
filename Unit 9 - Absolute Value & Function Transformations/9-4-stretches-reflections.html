Let's Begin

Steeper, flatter, flipped

Multiply every output of y = |x| by 3 and the V gets three times as steep. Multiply by −1 and it flips upside down. Multiply the input instead and the graph squeezes or stretches sideways. One number, several possible effects — today you'll learn to tell them apart.

Today you'll identify vertical and horizontal stretches, compressions, and reflections from equations, graphs, and tables.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Describe the effect of k·f(x): vertical stretch, compression, or reflection
  • 2Describe the effect of f(kx): horizontal compression, stretch, or reflection
  • 3Identify a transformation from an equation, a graph, or a table

Vocabulary

Multiplying outside versus inside

Vertical Stretch or Compression

k·f(x) multiplies every output by k. If |k| > 1 the graph stretches away from the x-axis; if 0 < |k| < 1 it compresses toward it. If k < 0 it also reflects over the x-axis.

💡Example: 3x² is narrower than x², ½x² is wider, and −x² opens down.
Horizontal Stretch or Compression

f(kx) multiplies every input by k. If |k| > 1 the graph compresses toward the y-axis; if 0 < |k| < 1 it stretches away. If k < 0 it also reflects over the y-axis.

💡Example: (2x)² reaches each output at half the x-value that x² needs.

How It Works

Outside changes outputs; inside changes inputs

1
Outside → vertical
k·f(x) changes the outputs: multiply every y-value by k.
2
Inside → horizontal
f(kx) changes the inputs, and acts the opposite way: k = 2 squeezes the graph to half its width.
3
A negative k reflects
−f(x) flips the graph over the x-axis. f(−x) flips it over the y-axis.
4
Check with a point
(2, 4) on y = x² becomes (2, 12) on y = 3x², and (1, 4) on y = (2x)².

💭 Notice: For |x| and lines through the origin, a vertical stretch and a horizontal compression can look identical: |2x| = 2|x|. For most functions they're different.

Real-World Context

Changes in rate or scale

Stretches model changes in rate or scale: a faster traveler's distance graph is steeper, and a scale model shrinks every measurement by the same factor.

Example: Walking at 3 mph, you've gone d(t) = 3t miles after t hours. Biking at 4 times that speed gives 4 · d(t) = 12t — the same line stretched vertically by a factor of 4.

Worked Example · Vertical

Comparing y = x², y = 2x², y = ½x², and y = −x²

Goal: Describe each graph compared with y = x².

1
y = 2x²
Every output doubles: (1, 1) becomes (1, 2). The graph is narrower.
2
y = ½x²
Every output is halved: (2, 4) becomes (2, 2). The graph is wider.
3
y = −x²
Every output changes sign: the graph reflects over the x-axis and opens down.
Gray: x². Blue: 2x². Green: ½x². Red: −x².

Worked Example · Horizontal

Comparing f(x) = x² − 4 and f(2x) = 4x² − 4

Goal: Describe how replacing x with 2x changes the graph.

1
Write f(2x)
f(2x) = (2x)² − 4 = 4x² − 4
2
Compare the zeros
f has zeros at x = ±2; f(2x) has zeros at x = ±1. Every point moves halfway to the y-axis.
3
The y-intercept stays put
Both pass through (0, −4): points on the y-axis don't move in a horizontal stretch or compression.
Gray: f(x) = x² − 4. Blue: f(2x) = 4x² − 4.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

f(x) = |x|. Describe g(x) = −3f(x).

Question 1 — What does the 3 do?

Here's why: The 3 multiplies the outputs, outside the function, so it's a vertical stretch: g is three times as steep.

Question 2 — What does the negative sign do?

Here's why: A negative outside the function flips the outputs, so the V opens down. A negative inside, f(−x), would reflect over the y-axis.

Why This Matters

Sketch any graph from its parent

With shifts, stretches, and reflections together, you can read any vertex-form equation as a set of moves on a parent graph — and sketch it without making a table.

This shows up everywhere

  • Sketching y = −2(x − 3)² + 5 from y = x² in three moves.
  • Comparing rates in linear models.
  • Recognizing transformations from a graph or table on the EOC.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
How does y = 4x² compare with y = x²?
Here's why: The 4 multiplies every output, so the graph stretches vertically and looks narrower.
Question 2
How does y = −|x| compare with y = |x|?
Here's why: The negative outside flips every output, so the V opens down. Reflecting |x| over the y-axis would leave it unchanged.
Question 3
f(x) = x². What is f(3x)?
Here's why: Replace x with 3x: (3x)² = 9x². 3x² is 3f(x), a different expression.
Question 4
The point (2, 6) is on the graph of f. Which point must be on y = ½f(x)?
Here's why: ½f(x) halves every output: (2, 6) → (2, 3). (4, 6) would be on f(½x), a horizontal stretch.
Question 5
Which equation is y = |x| reflected over the x-axis and shifted up 3?
Here's why: The negative outside reflects over the x-axis, and + 3 outside shifts up. |−x| equals |x|, so it doesn't reflect anything.
