Let's Begin

"Average rate of change" is just average speed, generalized to any function.

If you drove 120 miles in 2 hours, your average speed was 60 mph — total change in distance, divided by total change in time. Average rate of change is that exact same idea, applied to any function's output over any interval of its input.

Today you'll learn the formula, and use it on both linear and non-linear situations — because unlike slope, average rate of change works for every function type, not just straight lines.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Explain what average rate of change measures, and why it's an "average"
  • 2Calculate the average rate of change over a specified interval
  • 3Interpret the result in a real-world context, with correct units

Vocabulary

One formula, one word

Average Rate of Change

The average rate of change of a function f over the interval from x = a to x = b is: [f(b) − f(a)] / (b − a). It's the total change in output divided by the total change in input.

💡For a linear function, this number is always the same no matter which interval you pick — it's just the slope. For any other function type, it changes depending on the interval.

How It Works

Three numbers, one formula

The formula: average rate of change = [f(b) − f(a)] / (b − a)

1
Find f(a) and f(b)
Evaluate the function at the start and end of your interval — exactly like last lesson.
2
Subtract the outputs
f(b) − f(a) gives the total change in output over the interval.
3
Divide by the change in input
Divide by (b − a), the width of the interval, to get the average rate.

💭 Notice: this is exactly the slope formula, (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁), just written with function notation instead of coordinate points.

Real-World Context

Why "average" matters here

For a nonlinear function, the rate of change is usually different at every single point — a car speeds up and slows down constantly during a trip. Average rate of change smooths all of that into one number: the overall pace across the whole interval, even though the actual speed wandered above and below it the entire time.

Example: a plant's height might barely change in its first week, then shoot up in week three. The average rate of change over the full month tells you the overall growth pace — useful for comparing plants — even though no single week actually grew at exactly that rate.

Worked Example · From a Table

Finding average rate of change over an interval

Table: h(t) is a plant's height in cm at week t. h(1) = 4, h(5) = 20. Find the average rate of change from t = 1 to t = 5.

1
Identify a, b, f(a), f(b)
a = 1, b = 5, f(a) = h(1) = 4, f(b) = h(5) = 20
2
Apply the formula
[20 − 4] / [5 − 1] = 16 / 4 = 4
3
State the units and meaning
The plant grew an average of 4 cm per week between weeks 1 and 5.

Worked Example · Comparing Two Intervals

Rate of change isn't the same on every interval

Goal: Using f(0) = 2, f(2) = 6, f(4) = 26, compare the rate of change from 0 to 2 versus 2 to 4.

1
Interval 0 to 2
[6 − 2] / [2 − 0] = 4 / 2 = 2
2
Interval 2 to 4
[26 − 6] / [4 − 2] = 20 / 2 = 10

💭 Two different answers — and that's the point. Because this rate changed between intervals, f cannot be linear. This is actually another way to spot a nonlinear function, connecting back to Lesson 3.1.

Why This Matters

One formula, used across every function type

Average rate of change is one of the few tools in this course that works identically for linear, quadratic, exponential, or any other function — you'll reuse this exact formula in every future unit, on every new function type you meet.

This shows up everywhere

  • Sports: average speed over a race, computed from distance and time.
  • Economics: average growth rate of a stock's value over a period.
  • Science: average rate a chemical reaction proceeds, or a population grows.
