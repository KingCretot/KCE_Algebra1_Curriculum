Lesson 5.5 · Graphing Systems of Inequalities

Let's Begin

From one point to a whole region

A system of equations gives you exactly one point. A system of inequalities is different — instead of one exact answer, there's an entire shaded region of the coordinate plane where every single point works.

You already know how to graph a single linear inequality. Today you'll graph two at once and find where their shaded regions overlap.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Graph two linear inequalities on the same coordinate plane
  • 2Identify the overlapping shaded region as the solution set
  • 3Use a test point to verify a point lies in the solution set

Vocabulary

The words for "the shaded overlap"

Solution Region

The set of ALL points that satisfy every inequality in the system at once. On a graph, it's where the individual shaded regions overlap.

💡Unlike a system of equations, this region usually contains infinitely many points, not just one.
Boundary Line

The line that separates true from false for one inequality. Dashed for < or > (not included); solid for ≤ or ≥ (included).

💡Every system has two boundary lines — one per inequality.
Test Point

A point (often (0,0) when convenient) you substitute into an inequality to decide which side of the boundary line to shade.

💡If the test point makes it true, shade the side containing that point.

How It Works

Graph each inequality, then find the overlap

1
Graph the boundary line for the first inequality
Dashed for strict inequalities (<, >), solid for ≤ or ≥. Shade the correct side using a test point.
2
Graph the boundary line for the second inequality
On the SAME coordinate plane, using a different shading pattern or color so you can tell the two apart.
3
Identify where the two shaded regions overlap
That overlapping region — not either shading alone — is the solution set of the system.
4
Verify with a test point inside the overlap
Pick any point inside the overlap and check it makes BOTH original inequalities true.

💭 Watch for zero-coefficient lines: an inequality like x ≥ 3 or y < −2 graphs as a vertical or horizontal boundary line — don't force it into slope-intercept form where it doesn't belong.

Real-World Context

When you're bound by two limits at once

Systems of inequalities model real situations with more than one constraint operating simultaneously — a budget AND a time limit, a weight limit AND a size limit. The overlapping region shows every combination that satisfies both restrictions at the same time.

Example: A student has at most $30 to spend and needs to buy at least 4 total items at a store where pens cost $2 and notebooks cost $5. Two separate limits — a budget ceiling and a quantity floor — both have to hold at once.

Worked Example · Two Slanted Inequalities

Graphing y ≤ x + 2 and y > −x + 1

Goal: Shade the region satisfying both inequalities at once.

1
Graph y = x + 2 as a solid line
≤ includes the boundary, so the line is solid. Test (0,0): 0 ≤ 2 is true — shade below/on the line.
2
Graph y = −x + 1 as a dashed line
> does not include the boundary, so the line is dashed. Test (0,0): 0 > 1 is false — shade above the line, away from (0,0).
3
Identify the overlap
The wedge-shaped region above the dashed line AND on/below the solid line is the solution set.
4
Verify a point in the overlap
Try (0, 2): 2 ≤ 0+2 ✓ and 2 > −0+1 ✓ — both true, so (0,2) is in the solution set.

Worked Example · Zero-Coefficient Boundaries

Graphing x ≥ 2 and y < 5

Neither inequality has both x and y — each graphs as a straight vertical or horizontal line.

1
Graph x = 2 as a solid vertical line
≥ includes the boundary. Shade everything to the right, where x-values are 2 or greater.
2
Graph y = 5 as a dashed horizontal line
< excludes the boundary. Shade everything below, where y-values are less than 5.
3
Identify the overlap
A rectangular region to the right of x = 2 and below y = 5.

💭 Notice: "x ≥ 2" has no y in it at all — that's normal. It just means the boundary is vertical, and the rule applies no matter what y is.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

System: y < 2x + 3  &  y ≥ −1

Question 1 — What style is the boundary line for y ≥ −1?

Here's why: y ≥ −1 has no x, so it's a horizontal line at y = −1. The ≥ symbol includes the boundary itself, so the line is drawn solid, not dashed.

Question 2 — Does the point (0, 0) lie in the solution region for the full system?

Here's why: Check both: 0 < 2(0)+3 = 3 is true, and 0 ≥ −1 is true. Since (0,0) makes both inequalities true, it lies inside the overlapping solution region.

Why This Matters

Real decisions usually have more than one limit

Very few real-world decisions involve just one constraint. Budgets have both a spending ceiling and a minimum requirement; production has both a materials limit and a labor limit. Graphing a system of inequalities shows you every option that satisfies all the limits at once — not just one of them.

This shows up everywhere

  • Budgeting with both a spending cap and a minimum purchase requirement.
  • Manufacturing constrained by both raw materials and available labor hours.
  • Scheduling that must satisfy both a minimum staffing level and a maximum payroll.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
What does the solution to a system of two-variable inequalities look like on a graph?
Here's why: Unlike a system of equations, which has one exact point, a system of inequalities is satisfied by every point inside the region where both individual shadings overlap — typically infinitely many points.
Question 2
An inequality uses the < symbol. What style should its boundary line be?
Here's why: Strict inequalities (< or >) don't include the boundary itself, so the line is dashed to show points exactly on it don't count as solutions.
Question 3
How do you decide which side of a boundary line to shade?
Here's why: Plugging a test point (often (0,0) when it's not on the boundary) into the original inequality tells you directly whether that side makes the statement true — and that's the side you shade.
Question 4
What does the inequality x < 4 look like on a graph?
Here's why: With no y in the inequality, the boundary is vertical at x = 4. Since < is strict, the line is dashed, and since x-values must be less than 4, the shading goes to the left.
Question 5
A point lies inside the overlapping shaded region of a system. What must be true about it?
Here's why: The overlapping region is defined as every point that makes BOTH inequalities true simultaneously — that's the whole point of finding the overlap rather than either shaded region alone.
