Let's Begin

Not every math answer makes sense in real life

You've learned to build systems and solve them. Now comes the step that separates "getting an answer" from "solving the actual problem": once you have a solution, does it make sense given the real-world limits — the constraints — of the situation?

A math answer can be algebraically perfect and still be impossible in reality — negative people, fractional trucks, more hours than exist in a day.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Translate a real-world situation's limits into a system of equations or inequalities
  • 2Solve or graph the resulting system
  • 3Interpret each solution as viable or non-viable given the context

Vocabulary

The words for "does this answer actually work?"

Constraint

A real-world limit — a budget, a time limit, a minimum quantity — written as a linear equation or inequality that any valid solution must satisfy.

💡Most real problems have more than one constraint operating at the same time.
Viable Solution

A mathematically valid solution that ALSO makes sense in the real context — no negative counts, no fractional people, fits every stated limit.

💡A non-viable solution is math-correct but real-world impossible, like −3 tickets sold.

How It Works

From words to system to judgment call

1
Identify what each variable represents
Name your variables clearly — x = number of one thing, y = number of another.
2
Translate each stated limit into an equation or inequality
"At most" → ≤. "At least" → ≥. "Exactly" or a given total → =.
3
Solve or graph the resulting system
Use whichever method fits — substitution, elimination, or graphing the shaded region.
4
Check every solution against reality
Is it negative? Fractional when it needs to be a whole number? Outside a stated limit? If so, it's non-viable — even though it's mathematically correct.

💭 Notice: quantities of people, tickets, or whole items almost always require non-negative whole numbers — that real-world rule isn't written into the equation itself, so you have to apply it afterward.

Real-World Context

The whole point of building the system in the first place

Every earlier lesson in this unit taught you a tool. This lesson is where those tools actually get used the way they're used outside a classroom — someone with a real budget and a real set of needs, translating their situation into math, solving it, and then asking, "okay, but which of these answers can I actually use?"

Example: A student club has exactly $60 to spend on pizza and drinks for an event. Pizzas cost $12 each and drink packs cost $6 each. They need at least 2 pizzas. The system built from these limits will produce several mathematically valid combinations — but only whole-number, non-negative ones the club can actually order.

Worked Example · Writing the Constraints

Building the pizza & drinks system

Let p = number of pizzas, d = number of drink packs.

1
Translate the budget limit
"Exactly $60 to spend": 12p + 6d = 60
2
Translate the minimum pizza requirement
"At least 2 pizzas": p ≥ 2
3
Solve the budget equation for d
12p + 6d = 60 → d = (60 − 12p)/6 = 10 − 2p
4
Test whole-number values of p starting at 2
p=2 → d=6. p=3 → d=4. p=4 → d=2. p=5 → d=0. p=6 → d=−2 (stop here — negative).

Worked Example · Judging Viability

Which combinations can the club actually order?

Candidates found: (p=2, d=6), (p=3, d=4), (p=4, d=2), (p=5, d=0), (p=6, d=−2)

1
Check (2, 6), (3, 4), (4, 2), (5, 0)
All are whole numbers, non-negative, and satisfy p ≥ 2. These four are all viable.
2
Check (6, −2)
−2 drink packs is impossible — you can't buy a negative quantity. Non-viable, even though it satisfies the equation exactly.

💭 The takeaway: the equation 12p + 6d = 60 has infinitely many mathematical solutions, but only a handful are viable once you apply the real-world requirement that p and d are whole, non-negative numbers with p ≥ 2.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

Scenario: A vendor sells shirts (s) for $15 and hats (h) for $10. A customer has exactly $75 to spend and wants at least 3 total items.

Question 1 — Which system correctly represents these two constraints?

Here's why: "Exactly $75" becomes an equation: 15s + 10h = 75. "At least 3 total items" translates to ≥, not ≤ — the customer needs 3 or more, so s + h ≥ 3 is correct.

Question 2 — The solution s = 5, h = 0 satisfies the budget equation exactly. Is it viable?

Here's why: Buying zero of an item is perfectly realistic — it just means the customer didn't buy any hats. Since s=5, h=0 are both non-negative whole numbers, satisfy 15(5)+10(0)=75, and 5+0=5 ≥ 3, this solution is viable.

Why This Matters

This is what "using algebra" actually means

Every real decision that involves a budget, a schedule, or a set of resources runs through exactly this process: name the unknowns, translate the limits into math, solve, and then throw out any answer that doesn't actually work in reality. This is the step where Algebra 1 stops being an exercise and starts being a decision-making tool.

This shows up everywhere

  • Event and party planning within a fixed budget and a minimum headcount.
  • Small business inventory decisions constrained by cash on hand.
  • Any personal decision balancing a spending limit against a required minimum.

This is the final lesson of Unit 5 — you now have every tool needed to write, solve, graph, and judge systems of equations and inequalities.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
What is a "constraint" in a real-world systems problem?
Here's why: A constraint is a limit stated in the problem — a budget, a minimum, a maximum — rewritten in mathematical form so it can be part of the system being solved.
Question 2
The phrase "at most 20 items" translates to which inequality?
Here's why: "At most" sets an upper limit that can be reached but not exceeded, which is exactly what ≤ means — less than or equal to.
Question 3
A system solution gives x = −4 pizzas ordered. What is this solution?
Here's why: A solution can satisfy the algebra perfectly and still be non-viable if it violates a real-world condition — you cannot order a negative quantity of a physical item.
Question 4
Why might a fractional solution, like x = 2.5 trucks, be non-viable?
Here's why: 2.5 is a perfectly valid number mathematically, but in a context counting whole physical trucks, only whole-number answers make sense — the constraint comes from the situation, not the algebra.
Question 5
What is the first step in solving a real-world constraints problem?
Here's why: Without a clear definition of what x and y represent, it's impossible to correctly translate the word problem's limits into equations or to judge whether a final answer makes sense.
