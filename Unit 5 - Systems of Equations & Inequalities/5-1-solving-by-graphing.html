Let's Begin

Two equations, one shared answer

Every equation you've solved so far has had its own answer. Today, two linear equations exist on the same graph at the same time, and you're looking for the one point where both are true at once — the point where the two lines cross.

This is called a system of equations, and graphing is the most visual way to see what "solving a system" actually means.

By the end of today, you will be able to:

  • 1Graph two linear equations on the same coordinate plane
  • 2Identify the point of intersection as the system's solution
  • 3Check a solution by substituting it into both original equations

Vocabulary

The words for "two lines at once"

System of Equations

Two or more equations considered together, where you're looking for values that make all of them true at the same time.

💡In Algebra 1: systems are limited to exactly two linear equations.
Solution to a System

The ordered pair (x, y) that makes both equations true. On a graph, it's the point where the two lines intersect.

💡Check it: plug the point into both original equations — it must work in each one.

How It Works

Graph both lines, find where they meet

1
Get both equations into slope-intercept form
y = mx + b makes graphing fast: plot the y-intercept, then use the slope to find a second point.
2
Graph both lines on the same coordinate plane
Use a different color or style for each line if it helps you keep them straight.
3
Read the intersection point
The (x, y) coordinate where the lines cross is your solution.
4
Check by substituting
Plug the point into both original equations. If both come out true, you've confirmed the solution.

💭 Notice: graphing works for any system, but it's most reliable when the intersection lands on a nice whole-number point you can actually read off the grid.

Real-World Context

When two options meet in the middle

Systems show up anywhere you're comparing two options that both change over time or quantity — and you want to know exactly when they become equal. Two phone plans, two rental car deals, two savings accounts growing at different rates: each is its own linear equation, and the system tells you the exact point where they cost (or equal) the same.

Example: Gym A charges a $20 sign-up fee plus $15/month. Gym B charges no sign-up fee but $20/month. At some number of months, both gyms cost the same total — that's the system's solution, and it's the point where a smart consumer decides which gym is the better deal.

Worked Example · Two Equations

Solving y = 2x + 1 and y = −x + 4 by graphing

Goal: Find the point (x, y) that satisfies both equations.

1
Graph the first line
y = 2x + 1: y-intercept at (0, 1), slope 2 → from (0,1) go up 2, right 1 to (1, 3).
2
Graph the second line
y = −x + 4: y-intercept at (0, 4), slope −1 → from (0,4) go down 1, right 1 to (1, 3).
3
Read the intersection
Both lines pass through (1, 3) — that's where they cross.
4
Check in both equations
y = 2(1) + 1 = 3 ✓    y = −(1) + 4 = 3 ✓    Solution: (1, 3)

Worked Example · Real-World Context

When do the two gyms cost the same?

Gym A: y = 15x + 20     Gym B: y = 20x     (x = months, y = total cost)

1
Graph Gym A
y-intercept (0, 20), slope 15 → rises steeply but starts higher.
2
Graph Gym B
y-intercept (0, 0), slope 20 → starts at zero but rises even faster.
3
Find the intersection
The lines cross at (4, 80) — check: 15(4)+20 = 80, and 20(4) = 80. ✓

💭 Interpret it: at exactly 4 months, both gyms cost $80 total. Before month 4, Gym A is cheaper (lower starting fee wins). After month 4, Gym B costs more because its steeper monthly rate takes over.

Your Turn — Guided Practice

Now you try, with me right here

System: y = x + 2  &  y = 3x − 2

Question 1 — Graphing both lines, at what x-value do they cross?

Here's why: Graph both lines: y = x + 2 has y-intercept (0,2) and slope 1. y = 3x − 2 has y-intercept (0,−2) and slope 3. Tracing both, they meet at x = 2, where the first line gives y = 4 and the second gives y = 3(2)−2 = 4 — same point.

Question 2 — What is the full solution point?

Here's why: A solution to a system is always an ordered pair, not just the x-value. Substituting x = 2 into either equation gives y = 4, so the full solution is (2, 4). Check both: 4 = 2+2 ✓, and 4 = 3(2)−2 = 4 ✓.

Why This Matters

Graphing makes "the answer" visible

Solving a system algebraically (which you'll learn next) gets you to the same answer faster once equations get messy, but graphing is where the idea of "a solution to a system" actually clicks. It's not an abstract algebra trick — it's the literal point where two real, changing situations become equal.

This shows up everywhere

  • Break-even points between two pricing or cost plans.
  • Supply and demand curves crossing at market equilibrium.
  • Two vehicles traveling toward each other, meeting at a specific time and place.

Mini Knowledge Test

Show what you know — 5 quick questions

Question 1
What does the solution to a system of two linear equations represent on a graph?
Here's why: A system's solution must make both equations true simultaneously. On a graph, the only point that lies on both lines at once is the point where they cross.
Question 2
Two lines graphed together cross at (3, 7). What does this tell you?
Here's why: The intersection point IS the solution — its coordinates are the x and y values that make both original equations true at the same time.
Question 3
After graphing, how do you confirm your intersection point is actually correct?
Here's why: A reading error on the graph can give you a close-but-wrong point. Substituting into both equations is the only way to be certain the point truly satisfies the system.
Question 4
Plan A: y = 10x + 30. Plan B: y = 20x. What does the intersection point represent in context?
Here's why: In a cost-comparison context, x and y represent the same real quantities (months and total cost) in both equations, so the intersection is exactly the moment the two totals become equal.
Question 5
Why is slope-intercept form especially useful before graphing a system?
Here's why: y = mx + b hands you your starting point (b) and your direction (m) immediately, which is exactly what you need to plot a line quickly and accurately by hand.
